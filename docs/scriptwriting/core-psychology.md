# Core psychology & craft (shared foundation)

This is the canonical reference. `short-form-template.md` and
`long-form-template.md` both build on top of everything here — they should
never restate these principles at length, only apply them. If a template
looks like it's re-explaining something below, that's drift; fix it by
deleting the restatement and pointing back here instead.

---

## 1. The master principle: expectations vs. reality

Content psychology reduces to one thing: what the viewer expected going in,
versus what they actually got.

- **Reality beats expectations** → viewer is pleasantly surprised, keeps
  watching, shares it.
- **Expectations beat reality** → viewer feels let down, leaves.

Every technique below is just a tactic for engineering that gap in your
favor. Before applying any of them, know what expectation you're setting —
you can't beat an expectation you haven't defined.

## 2. Click confirmation

The first lines of any video must **confirm** the expectation set by the
title, then ideally **exceed** it immediately.

- Confirm: if the title promises X, the opening says/shows X right away.
- Exceed: add something the title didn't promise ("I spent 100 hours inside
  the pyramids" → confirmed by showing the pyramids; exceeded by adding
  "with unprecedented access no one's had before").

Skipping confirmation (opening on something unrelated to the title) is the
single fastest way to lose a viewer who clicked in good faith.

## 3. Packaging: idea, title, story lens

Packaging happens **before** any script gets written — it's the input the
script serves, not a decoration added after.

- **Idea**: one line describing the pain point or curiosity loop the video
  resolves. If this isn't something the target viewer actually wants
  answered, no amount of scriptcraft saves it.
- **Title**: sets the expectation the whole video is judged against. Small
  wording changes shift the expectation, so lock the title before writing
  the intro — you can't engineer a confirmation against a target that's
  still moving.
- **Story lens** (your unique angle): in a saturated topic, the topic alone
  isn't enough differentiation. Picture a beam of light — everyone covering
  the same story is looking at the same raw beam. Your lens is the prism
  that makes what you show look different from what everyone else showed.
  The less common the lens, the more of a category-of-one the video becomes.
  Pick the lens *before* writing, same stage as idea/title.
- Thumbnail can stay loose while you write — nailing it too early slows down
  the parts (idea/title/lens) that actually gate whether the script is worth
  writing.

## 4. The 4-part hook skeleton

The reusable shape for opening any video, regardless of length. Full-length
version below; `short-form-template.md` shows the same 4 beats compressed to
1–2 lines.

1. **Context** — state plainly what this video is about, so the viewer can
   confirm the click.
2. **Common belief** — restate the conventional wisdom on the topic. Do this
   even though you're about to contradict it: restating it first signals
   "I understand what you already believe," which builds the trust needed
   for the next beat to land, and it's the only way to set up a contrast.
3. **Contrarian take** — contradict the common belief with your actual
   position. This is the moment reality starts beating expectations: the
   viewer expected another rehash of the common belief and got a different
   angle instead.
4. **Proof + plan** — establish why they should trust this contrarian take
   (credentials, results, evidence), and give an ordered plan for what's
   coming. Order between proof and plan is flexible; both need to land
   before the hook is done.

## 5. Universal craft rules

These apply to every sentence you write, in any format.

### The Dance — but/therefore, never and-then

Beats in a story connect with **but** or **therefore**, never **and then**.
"And then" just piles on detail after detail with no causality, and the
viewer's attention drifts. "But" and "therefore" each create a small
open-loop/close-loop moment (conflict → resolution → new conflict), which is
what actually holds attention across a sequence of beats.

Tactically: write your beats out, and check that the word connecting each
pair could honestly be "but" or "therefore." If the only honest connector is
"and then," the beat is decoration, not story — cut it or find the
causality.

This applies wherever you have more than one beat in sequence: transitions
between long-form body points, or the internal beats of a short-form loop.

### Rhythm — vary sentence length on purpose

A run of same-length sentences reads as monotonous and subconsciously bores
the listener, regardless of how good the content is — this is a purely
mechanical effect, independent of what you're saying. Mix short, medium, and
long sentences so the read has a variable cadence ("the writing sings"
rather than droning).

Practical check: lay out your script one sentence per line. If the ends of
the lines form a flat edge (everything terminating around the same column),
you don't have enough length variation.

### Tone — write to one person, not an audience

The most-watched creators read as conversational, not broadcast-at-you. The
craft trick: write (and film) as if speaking to one specific close friend,
not "an audience." This dissolves the viewer's subconscious "am I being sold
to" guard and lets them just be in the conversation. Improves with reps;
literally aiming your framing at one named/pictured person accelerates it.

### Visual hook > audio-only hook

A hook paired with a concrete visual is dramatically more effective than
words alone — eyes process faster than ears, so give viewers something to
look at and react to that reinforces what's being said, not just a talking
mouth. "Show while you tell" applies to any hook, but matters most wherever
the window to earn attention is shortest.
